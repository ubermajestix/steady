🎈 Big Feelings Helper

For when a feeling gets really, really big

🌊

Did a feeling just get huge?

Like when someone says no, or you get in trouble, or a friend seems mad, and your whole body feels like a giant wave?

🚨

Step 1: It's a false alarm

Your body has an alarm. Right now it's going REALLY loud, like there's a big emergency.

But guess what? You are safe. The feeling is huge, but it can't hurt you. It's a false alarm, and it always gets smaller.

Say it out loud: "This is a big feeling. It will get smaller."

🎈

Step 2: Blow up the balloon

Tap start

Breathe IN as it grows big. Breathe OUT as it shrinks. Do it 4 times.

⏳

Step 3: Wait before you do a big thing

When the feeling is huge, it wants you to yell, run, or say something mean. That's the alarm talking, not you.

Wait a little. The wave always goes down. Then you get to choose what to do, not the alarm.

🤔

Step 4: Was it as bad as it felt?

Big feelings tell big stories. Let's check:

Feeling left out is not the same as being left out. A lot of the time, it's just two people wanting different things.

🩹

Step 5: Making it okay again

Sometimes a big feeling makes us say something mean or push someone away. If that happened, you can fix it.

Saying "I'm sorry" doesn't mean you're bad. It's how you make things okay again.

You can say sorry and tell them what you were feeling. Sorry first, then the story.

💛

You did it

You rode the wave. That takes practice, and every time you do it, it gets easier.

One more thing: talking to a grown-up who loves you always helps. You don't have to handle big feelings alone.

🧠

Why do feelings get SO big?

Some brains have a loud alarm. When someone says no, or you think a friend is upset, the alarm goes off like it's a real emergency, even when you're totally safe.

It's not your fault, and it doesn't mean anything is wrong with you. Lots of people have a loud alarm. You can teach it to be a little quieter, a little at a time.

The trick every time: notice it, breathe, wait, then check the story.

A note for grown-ups

This pairs with the adult guide. The most helpful things you can do in the moment: get calm yourself first, name the feeling out loud ("that felt really unfair, huh"), let the feeling be okay, and help work through it rather than fixing or lecturing. Solve the problem after your child has come back down, not during. Catch them being good and keep corrections gentle and few. If big reactions are frequent and getting in the way of friendships, school, or how your child feels about themselves, ask their pediatrician; behavioral parent training is a well-evidenced first step. This is a self-help tool, not medical advice.
